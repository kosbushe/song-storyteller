# Multilingual research

## Research ladder

Search in this order, adapting to the work:

1. **Original language and script:** official credits, local interviews, broadcaster archives, national libraries, collecting societies, contemporary press, and scholarship from the song's own culture.
2. **Response language:** identify what the intended reader is likely to misunderstand and whether a local legend or translation has shaped reception.
3. **Bridge language:** use English or another well-documented language to find international interviews, production history, reissues, and reputable translations.
4. **Back to the original:** verify any bridge-language claim against an original-language source when possible.

Do not treat an English-language summary as more authoritative merely because it is accessible. Local primary evidence outranks distant repetition.

## Search variants

Try:

- original title, romanization, established translated title, and common misspellings;
- artist name in original and Latin scripts;
- writer, composer, producer, arranger, translator, and cover performer names;
- terms equivalent to interview, lyrics, meaning, recording, studio, oral history, archive, liner notes, and controversy in the relevant language;
- date-bounded searches near the original release, not only anniversary retrospectives.

Track whether different spellings point to the same person or work. Never silently merge distinct songs with similar translated titles.

## Translation and interpretation

- Identify the lyric's register: formal, conversational, archaic, sacred, slang, regional, or deliberately mixed.
- Explain pronouns, grammatical gender, honorifics, omitted subjects, tense, aspect, and word order only when they alter the relationship or story.
- Preserve code-switching and untranslatable terms when translation would erase social meaning; explain them briefly.
- Treat idioms as actions or intentions, not word puzzles. Give the natural meaning before literal components.
- When wordplay permits several readings, name the alternatives and the evidence for each.
- Distinguish an official adaptation from a literal translation. A new lyric set to the same melody may tell a different story.
- Use transliteration sparingly and follow a recognized system when precision matters.

## Special cases

### Traditional, folk, and oral songs

Separate the earliest documented version from claims of ancient origin. Name the collecting, transcription, publication, or field-recording history. Avoid assigning a modern national identity to a song whose circulation crosses borders unless evidence supports it.

### Covers, remixes, and adaptations

Research both the underlying composition and the requested recording. Explain what the later artist changed in words, narrator, arrangement, genre, gender, politics, or audience. Do not credit the cover performer with writing the original.

### Instrumentals

Look for a documented program, commission, title origin, performance tradition, film or ritual use, and composer's statements. Describe audible structure as analysis, not as a hidden plot.

### Sacred, political, and contested material

Use sources from more than one relevant community. Attribute contested names, borders, identities, translations, and interpretations. Do not turn a living dispute into decorative drama.

### Sparse evidence

Say what is unknown. A precise account of what can and cannot be established is better than a universal story assembled from weak sources.

## Copyright boundary

Do not reproduce full lyrics, paywalled articles, or long passages. Quote the shortest phrase required for linguistic analysis and paraphrase the rest. A request for a full translation of non-user-provided lyrics is outside this skill's editorial format; offer a meaning-focused summary instead.
