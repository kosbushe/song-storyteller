# Editorial standard

## Promise

Make the reader want to replay the song immediately and notice something previously missed. Let surprise come from verified context, not exaggeration.

## Source hierarchy

Prefer sources in this order:

1. Direct interviews with writers, performers, producers, engineers, musicians, translators, and adapters.
2. Official artist, label, publisher, studio, broadcaster, museum, library, collecting-society, or industry archives.
3. Liner notes, authorized biographies, session documentation, and archived radio or television programs.
4. Publications with named authors, editorial accountability, and music expertise.
5. Specialist databases for dates, credits, charts, awards, and discography.
6. Fan archives, forums, aggregators, and social posts only as leads to stronger evidence.

Do not trust a page merely because it ranks highly or repeats a familiar story. If credible sources disagree, show the disagreement.

## Attribution and evidence

- Name whose version is being presented: writer, performer, producer, biographer, critic, scholar, translator, or audience.
- Consider when a recollection was recorded. A late interview may conflict with evidence from the release period.
- Do not join two confirmed facts with an unverified causal claim.
- Do not turn the absence of a denial into confirmation.
- Use official or specialist sources for dates, credits, chart positions, sales, and awards.
- When the primary source is inaccessible, cite the reliable publication quoting it and make that mediation clear.
- Treat many sites copying one origin as one source, not independent confirmation.

## Three kinds of meaning

Keep these separate:

1. **Creator testimony:** a writer or performer directly explains the origin or intention.
2. **Textual and musical analysis:** an interpretation supported by lyrics, composition, arrangement, delivery, or narrative point of view.
3. **Cultural reading:** a meaning acquired through audiences, politics, film, ritual, covers, memes, or historical events.

These layers may conflict. That conflict often creates the best story. A creator's account is evidence of intention, not ownership of every later meaning.

## Meaningful surprise

A strong turn should do at least one of these:

- change the assumed speaker, addressee, or situation;
- explain a contradiction between the sound and the words;
- reveal what an idiom, dialect, translation, or cultural reference hides;
- correct a durable legend;
- show how a technical choice became part of the meaning;
- connect a private origin to a larger cultural life;
- reveal how a cover or adaptation transformed the work.

A celebrity cameo, rare demo, award, or chart record is not a turn unless it changes the song's meaning or sound.

## Links

Place each link after the sentence or paragraph it supports. Use a descriptive label. Do not flood every sentence with links, but support every claim on which the story depends.

Attribute disputed material in prose: "the producer later recalled," "the biographer argues," or "contemporary coverage reported." A link does not erase uncertainty.
