# Literary voice

## Reader contract

The reader did not ask for a Wikipedia recap. They want to understand a song they have heard many times and discover one verified story that changes the next listen.

When language or cultural distance hides the meaning, "Do you understand what this song is really about?" is an editorial promise, not clickbait. Answer in the first two paragraphs. Build curiosity around how the song arrived there and why its sound may be misleading.

## Voice

- Sound like a knowledgeable music columnist sitting beside the reader and replaying the recording.
- Prefer concrete nouns and precise verbs. Name the person who wrote, sang, changed, refused, remembered, or misremembered something.
- Vary sentence length and shape. One short sentence may land; several fragments in a row manufacture drama.
- Make details do two jobs: establish a fact and alter the reader's understanding of a person, lyric, performance, or sound.
- Trust the reader. Do not explain a strong fact again after presenting it.
- Use a metaphor once, then leave it behind.
- Let three to five strong inline sources support the story without turning it into a research memo.
- State the central thesis fully at most twice: in the opening and in the conclusion. The middle must prove it, not paraphrase it.

## Remove before publishing

Delete:

- bureaucratic and academic throat-clearing;
- repeated "not X but Y" reversals;
- negative countdowns such as "not revenge, not grief, but freedom";
- questions immediately answered for fake suspense;
- stacked fragments used as artificial profundity;
- promises such as "here is where it gets interesting" or "what few people know";
- vague authorities such as "experts say" and "many critics believe";
- labels for the writer's own machinery, including "the key insight" and "the real twist";
- identical punch-line endings on every paragraph;
- excessive dashes, bold-first lists, and ornamental formatting;
- biography, charts, awards, and trivia that do not change this song.

One instance of a rhetorical pattern may be natural. Repetition turns a voice into a template.

After the opening meaning question, do not use another self-answered question. Avoid one-line paragraphs that exist only to create a dramatic pause.

## Editing pass

Ask:

1. Can a reader who does not know the song's language explain its situation after two paragraphs?
2. Does every later paragraph add a scene, piece of evidence, linguistic insight, or sound?
3. Can every strong factual claim be traced to a named source?
4. Is there at least one concrete scene or audible detail?
5. Does the prose sound like a specific human voice when read aloud in the response language?
6. Does the middle repeat the thesis instead of proving it?

If an answer is no, revise. Then read the opening and ending aloud and cut wherever the voice catches.
